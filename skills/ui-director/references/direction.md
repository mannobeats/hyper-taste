# Direction: pages that sell

Read this for landing pages, launch and product pages, portfolios, pricing pages and any site
that has to make someone act. There is no template page here on purpose: the layout, the
section order, the palette and the type come from this product every time. Two products built
with this skill should never share a layout.

## Find the idea

The visitor arrives asking three things: what is this, is it for me, what do I click. A great
page answers them in the first screen, and makes them feel something while it does.

- **Start from the product's world, not its category.** List the objects, places, rituals,
  materials and moments around the product. A climbing gym has the route wall, the chalk bag,
  the logbook, the topo map. A bank has none of these, so find what its customers hold: the
  receipt, the salary day, the rent. The idea is one of these things, and it decides the first
  screen, the order, the palette and the type.
- **Name the obvious version first,** then leave it. It's the page anyone would build: the
  category's usual first screen, its stock palette (cream and print red for anything historical,
  navy for finance, green for climate, purple for AI, black and one orange for premium), the
  usual order. `SKILL.md` "Model habits" is the obvious version of today's models.
- **Three directions that differ in structure,** not only in color. Each one puts the first
  screen together differently: where the words sit, how big the visual is, what the eye meets
  first.
- **The signature** is the one thing only this product could show: a live demo of its core
  action, a surprising number, a photograph, an interaction. It gets the most space and care and
  lives on the first screen, or is the first thing you scroll into. If the page would fit another
  product unchanged, keep looking.
- **What worked in testing:** a headline that is the booking control ("Board in [Amsterdam] after
  dinner. Wake up in [Vienna]"), a section drawing of a quay wall that clears as you scroll, an
  architect's sections with a sun you can drag. Each came from the product's own world and put
  the visitor's hands on it in the first screen. What failed: a strong idea placed below a safe
  photo-and-headline first screen. Don't reuse these ideas; find the one this
  product's world offers.

## The first screen

It needs a short, concrete headline and at most one supporting sentence; one primary action
with the line that lowers its risk, plus at most one secondary link; and the visual, or the
clear start of it.

These don't have to sit together in one block. The headline can be the image, the line can sit
at the bottom of the screen, and the action can live in the nav and at the end of the first
scene. Decide what carries the screen:

- **The thing itself,** shown large: the product, the interface, the work.
- **A statement:** type alone, the visual arriving as you scroll.
- **An image or scene** that sets the mood, the words on a calm area of it.
- **Something to try:** the visitor uses it right there (type, drag, toggle, play).
- **The data:** a number, a live list, a map.

Then decide where the words sit and how big the visual is:

- centered, over or under the visual
- anchored bottom-left or bottom-right over a full-bleed image
- running the full width, with the product breaking into it or standing in front of it
- after the visual: it fills the first screen and the words arrive as you scroll
- a narrow column of words against a huge visual that bleeds off the edge
- on the right, when the visual reads best from the left

Three layouts are what everyone reaches for first: words on one side and the picture on the
other, a centered headline over a big product view, and a huge headline top-left with a small
paragraph and buttons beside it and the product framed below. Use one only when you can say why
it beats the others for this product.

On a 1280×720 laptop the first screen shows the headline, the line under it, the button and
part of the visual. Check it at that size.

## Make it premium

Looking good gets a product picked; evidence that it's good gets it called premium. A clean
page with a centered headline is the floor, not the goal.

- **One real object, given the stage:** the product, the thing it makes, or its actual output,
  large and lit with care: a loaf shot like a still life, the report the tool writes, the app
  standing in the room where it's used.
- **Depth:** at least two planes. A photograph behind, the product in front, one detail (a
  notification, a note) crossing an edge.
- **A detail only an expert would add:** guide lines on the real font metrics, a live "on the
  shelf now" strip, a chart of real traffic with the hole the bug would punch in it.
- **Type chosen and set on purpose:** a face from the product's world, larger than feels
  comfortable. A light weight at a huge size reads expensive.
- **Restraint around it:** one accent, wide margins, small quiet text, nothing decorative that
  doesn't carry the idea.

## Order the page by the visitor's questions

The stock order is hero, logos, three steps, feature grid, testimonial, pricing, FAQ, final call
to action. Don't start there. Order the sections by what this visitor asks next, and drop the
ones they don't ask:

- a developer tool goes straight from the first screen into a working demo
- a café leads with today's menu and the map
- a camera or drone shows what it sees first, then the object, then the specs one line at a time
- a portfolio shows the work before it talks about the studio

Give each section its own form: a full-bleed image, a live piece of UI, a list, one huge number,
a big quote, a table, a map, a quiet pause. Never the same composition three times in a row. With
more than four features, visuals lead and text supports.

## Make it convert

A landing page is a salesperson. Before calling it done, review it as a business: what could stop
someone from acting?

- **The headline says what it is and what the visitor gets** ("Workout plans that adapt to your
  week", not "Find your rhythm"). The line under it says how, and for whom.
- **One action, one label, everywhere,** nav included: "Order Lumo", not "Buy", "Shop" and "Get
  yours" on one page. Drop secondary buttons that compete.
- **Say what clicking costs:** one quiet line by the button ("100 nights to try it. Free returns.").
- **Ask for as little as possible.** The form asks only what's needed to start, often just an
  email, then shows the next step straight away.
- **Keep the action in reach.** On long pages the nav sticks with the button in it (on phones, a
  bottom bar once the first screen has passed), and the action comes back after each big proof.
- **Show the outcome,** one concrete result ("78% woke up less groggy after two weeks"), where it
  convinces most.
- **Proof that feels real:** a rating with its count, named customers, a quote with a name, a
  role and a specific detail. Mark invented numbers as sample data.
- **Portfolios convert through the work:** big, specific images of each project, what it was
  like to be there, a short account of how the studio works, and one way to get in touch.

## Flavor

Write it down before styling. It changes color, radius, type, imagery and motion; the hard bans
hold in every flavor.

- **Friendly:** tinted surfaces, illustrations or characters, rounder corners (12–20px), bigger
  buttons, playful motion.
- **Premium:** restrained color, tight radius (4–10px), graded photography, precise motion, and
  generous scale. Premium is confidence, not smallness.
- **Technical:** dense, mono on code and data only, the real interface as the imagery.
- **Editorial:** large type with tight tracking, a 60–70ch measure, photography with one grade,
  asymmetric crops, a display face from the subject's world when you can say why.
- **Bold:** oversized type that becomes the image, strong color, full-bleed imagery, confident
  scroll scenes, rules broken on purpose.

## Type is art direction

On pages that sell, type carries as much of the mood as the images:

- Scale hard: one headline much bigger than anything else, small quiet text around it.
- Choose weight and width on purpose. A light weight at a huge size, a condensed face for an
  industrial product, a rounded face for a children's app: each with one line of why.
- Tracking −0.01 to −0.04em on big type, never tighter; line height 0.95–1.1 on headlines.
- Two or three lines per headline at `clamp()` sizes, `text-wrap: balance`.

## Pricing

- Two or three plans. Mark the recommended one with the darker button, not a taller card, a
  glowing border or a "Most popular" pill.
- One line of what's different per plan, 3–5 concrete items each. Say what's free and how
  billing works.

## Finishing touches

- **Footer:** logo, a few links, legal. No four-column link farm for a small product.
- **Meta:** title, description, an Open Graph image, a favicon.
- **404:** a moment of personality in the product's voice, and a clear way back.
- **Analytics** (live sites): tag the primary button and the form submit, and load trackers only
  after consent where the law asks for it.
